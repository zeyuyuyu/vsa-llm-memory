What the paper may say (registered reading, "H1-B46 passes"): at equal bits, in the few-values low-budget region
(V in {2, 16}, 4-16 bits per fact), the superposed store beats the best dev-chosen classical memory (key-free sketches,
static functions, Bloom filters, SDM, or the per-fact index) on frozen hidden-state codes of two LLM families: Qwen3-8B
(mean of layer 9; +6.8 points) and Granite-3.3-8B-Instruct (mean of layer 30; +6.1 points), with the layer and pooling of
each chosen by an alias-retrieval criterion, not by the memory comparison; the margin also holds against the
test-selected competitor (+5.7 / +4.8). In the same breath: no win on the e5 sentence encoder (tie, +0.6); the store
loses with 1,024 values (-3.9 / -4.6) and is not ahead at 1-2 or 32-64 bits per fact on the Granite codes.
What it must not say: that the win holds for LLM codes in general, for every reader, or for neural codes as such (two of
three readers; the e5 tie is the counterexample; on Granite the three M 256, V 16 cells of R1 are ties); that the
second family was chosen to favour the store (it was not, but the paper should name the criterion); that the Qwen3-8B
RH advantage replicates (it does not: RH is a tie on Granite).
Mechanism (descriptive): after centring, Granite's alias-label geometry resembles Qwen3-8B's (alias-label cosine median
0.53 vs 0.51; an alias shares its label's 8-bit SimHash signature 8% of the time in both, 12% for e5), so exact-signature
structures rarely match and the store's graded-similarity read keeps its edge; on e5 coarse signatures match more often.
