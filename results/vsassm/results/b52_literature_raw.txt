TOPIC 1. Layer choice for reading discrete symbol identity from frozen LLM states
======================================================================

(a) Intermediate vs final layers: token identity / surface information

T1-1  Voita, Sennrich, Titov (2019). "The Bottom-up Evolution of Representations in the Transformer:
      A Study with Machine Translation and Language Modeling Objectives." EMNLP 2019 (venue from memory).
      arXiv:1909.01380. VERIFIED (abstract).
      Says: with CCA and mutual-information estimators, in left-to-right LMs "information about the past
      ... gets vanished and predictions about the future get formed" going bottom to top; MLM partially
      forgets token identity then reconstructs it. Most direct support for "final layer of a causal LM is
      specialised to next-token prediction, so current-token identity is better read lower down".

T1-2  Brunner, Liu, Pascual, Richter, Ciaramita, Wattenhofer (2020). "On Identifiability in
      Transformers." ICLR 2020 (venue from memory). arXiv:1908.04211. VERIFIED (abstract).
      Says: input tokens largely retain their identity across the model, identity information is mainly
      in the angle of the embeddings and "gradually decreases with depth" (BERT). Supports reading
      identity below the top, and angle-based (cosine) read-out.

T1-3  Skean, Arefin, Zhao, Patel, Naghiyev, LeCun, et al. (2025). "Layer by Layer: Uncovering Hidden
      Representations in Language Models." arXiv:2502.02013 (ICML 2025, venue from memory). VERIFIED
      (abstract). Says: intermediate layers "consistently provide stronger features" than the last layer
      on 32 text-embedding tasks across transformers and SSMs; explained by a compression/signal-
      preservation trade-off. Caveat: tasks are semantic embedding tasks, not exact token identity.

T1-4  Tenney, Das, Pavlick (2019). "BERT Rediscovers the Classical NLP Pipeline." ACL 2019, pp. 4593-4601.
      arXiv:1905.05950. VERIFIED (abstract + journal_ref). Says: lower-level information (POS) is
      localised in lower layers and higher-level (coreference) higher up, in pipeline order. Encoder-only;
      classic citation for "surface/lexical info lives lower".

T1-5  Liu, Gardner, Belinkov, Peters, Smith (2019). "Linguistic Knowledge and Transferability of
      Contextual Representations." NAACL 2019 (venue from memory). arXiv:1903.08855. VERIFIED (abstract).
      Says: linear probes on frozen layers; higher RNN layers are more task-specific, while transformer
      layers do not show a monotonic trend; middle layers are often the most transferable.

T1-6  Belrose, Ostrovsky, McKinney, Furman, Smith, Halawi, et al. (2023). "Eliciting Latent Predictions
      from Transformers with the Tuned Lens." arXiv:2303.08112. VERIFIED (abstract).
      Says: trains an affine probe per block of a frozen LM to decode every hidden state to a vocabulary
      distribution; more reliable than the earlier "logit lens" (nostalgebraist 2020, LessWrong blog --
      UNVERIFIED, seen only as a secondary mention here). Relevant precedent for per-layer affine
      read-outs on frozen LMs (our naming head is the same kind of object).

T1-7  Feucht, Atkinson, Wallace, Bau (2024). "Token Erasure as a Footprint of Implicit Vocabulary Items
      in LLMs." arXiv:2406.20086 (EMNLP 2024, venue from memory). VERIFIED (abstract).
      Says: in Llama-2-7b / Llama-3-8B, the last-token representation of multi-token words and named
      entities shows an "erasure" effect where information about previous and current tokens is rapidly
      forgotten in EARLY layers. Caution for our hex/decimal strings: a multi-token symbol's identity is
      re-assembled into a word-like unit, so the best read-out layer depends on whether one reads
      per-token or whole-symbol identity.

(b) How single-digit-tokenised LLMs represent multi-digit numbers

T1-8  Levy, Geva (2024). "Language Models Encode Numbers Using Digit Representations in Base 10."
      arXiv:2410.11781 (NAACL 2025, venue from memory). VERIFIED (abstract).
      Says: probing + causal interventions show LLMs represent numbers with individual circular
      representations PER DIGIT in base 10, not as a single value; explains digit-distributed errors.
      Supports a digit-wise (compositional) code that a linear naming head can read at mid layers.

T1-9  Zhu, Dai, Sui (2024). "Language Models Encode the Value of Numbers Linearly." arXiv:2401.03735
      (COLING 2025, venue from memory). VERIFIED (abstract).
      Says: linear probes read input-number values from hidden states "on different layers"; calculation
      results are stored similarly and can be steered by vector addition.

T1-10 Zhou, Fu, Sharan, Jia (2024). "Pre-trained Large Language Models Use Fourier Features to Compute
      Addition." arXiv:2406.03445 (NeurIPS 2024, venue from memory). VERIFIED (abstract).
      Says: numbers are represented by features sparse in the frequency domain (low freq = magnitude,
      high freq = mod-k such as parity); pre-training is crucial for this.

T1-11 Singh, Strouse (2024). "Tokenization counts: the impact of tokenization on arithmetic in frontier
      LLMs." arXiv:2402.14903. VERIFIED (abstract).
      Says: LLaMA/PaLM use single-digit tokenisation, GPT-3.5/4 use 1-3 digit tokens; left-to-right vs
      right-to-left chunking changes arithmetic accuracy markedly and errors are stereotyped. Supports
      noting that Qwen3's single-digit tokenisation shapes what a read-out head has to compose.

(c) Anisotropy / common direction and centring / whitening fixes

T1-12 Ethayarajh (2019). "How Contextual are Contextualized Word Representations? Comparing the Geometry
      of BERT, ELMo, and GPT-2 Embeddings." EMNLP-IJCNLP 2019 (venue from memory). arXiv:1909.00512.
      VERIFIED (abstract). Says: representations are not isotropic in ANY layer; same-word self-similarity
      is much lower in upper layers (upper layers more context-specific); <5% of variance explained by a
      static embedding.

T1-13 Mu, Bhat, Viswanath (2018). "All-but-the-Top: Simple and Effective Postprocessing for Word
      Representations." arXiv:1702.01417 (ICLR 2018, venue from memory). VERIFIED (abstract).
      Says: removing the common mean vector and a few dominating directions makes embeddings stronger
      on lexical and sentence tasks. Direct precedent for our centring step.

T1-14 Su, Cao, Liu, Ou (2021). "Whitening Sentence Representations for Better Semantics and Faster
      Retrieval." arXiv:2103.15316. VERIFIED (abstract).
      Says: anisotropy is a bottleneck for BERT sentence vectors; plain whitening restores isotropy, is
      competitive with flow-based fixes, and reduces dimension.

T1-15 Timkey, van Schijndel (2021). "All Bark and No Bite: Rogue Dimensions in Transformer Language Models
      Obscure Representational Quality." arXiv:2109.04404 (EMNLP 2021, venue from memory). VERIFIED
      (abstract). Says: 1-3 "rogue" dimensions dominate cosine/Euclidean similarity; simple
      standardisation corrects this and reveals representational quality.

T1-16 Sun, Chen, Kolter, Liu (2024). "Massive Activations in Large Language Models." arXiv:2402.17762
      (COLM 2024, venue from memory). VERIFIED (abstract).
      Says: a very few activations are ~100,000x larger than the rest, largely input-independent, and act
      as bias terms. An input-independent common component of the kind centring removes.

T1-17 Razzhigaev, Mikhalchuk, Goncharova, Oseledets, Dimitrov, Kuznetsov (2023). "The Shape of Learning:
      Anisotropy and Intrinsic Dimensions in Transformer-Based Models." arXiv:2311.05928 (EACL 2024
      findings?, venue from memory). VERIFIED (abstract).
      Says: in DECODERS the anisotropy profile is bell-shaped with the HIGHEST anisotropy in the MIDDLE
      layers (encoders are flatter). NUANCE for us: mid-layer read-out is not explained by lower
      anisotropy there; anisotropy is if anything higher at mid depth, so centring/whitening should be
      tried at layers 9/27 as well, not only at the final layer.

T1-18 Godey, de la Clergerie, Sagot (2024). "Anisotropy Is Inherent to Self-Attention in Transformers."
      arXiv:2401.12143 (EACL 2024, venue from memory). VERIFIED (abstract).
      Says: anisotropy appears even where the long-tailed cross-entropy explanation should not apply and
      across modalities; argues it is inherent to Transformers.

Not found: a paper that specifically probes exact identity of arbitrary multi-token strings (hex/IDs)
across layers of a Qwen/Llama-class decoder. T1-1, T1-2, T1-7 are the closest.

TOPIC 2. Variance from random seeds / random maps and configuration selection
======================================================================

T2-1  Bouthillier, Delaunay, Bronzi, Trofimov, Nichyporuk, Szeto, et al. (2021). "Accounting for Variance
      in Machine Learning Benchmarks." arXiv:2103.03098 (MLSys 2021, venue from memory). VERIFIED
      (abstract). Says: variance from data sampling, parameter initialisation and HYPERPARAMETER CHOICE
      markedly changes conclusions; randomising more sources of variation (including the HP search)
      approaches the ideal estimator at 51x lower cost than full re-runs; gives recommendations.
      Closest match to "re-select the configuration on dev for each new random map".

T2-2  Dodge, Ilharco, Schwartz, Farhadi, Hajishirzi, Smith (2020). "Fine-Tuning Pretrained Language
      Models: Weight Initializations, Data Orders, and Early Stopping." arXiv:2002.06305. VERIFIED
      (abstract). Says: hundreds of BERT fine-tunes varying only the seed give substantially different
      results; weight init and data order contribute comparably; best-of-N grows with trials.

T2-3  Reimers, Gurevych (2017). "Reporting Score Distributions Makes a Difference: Performance Study of
      LSTM-networks for Sequence Tagging." arXiv:1707.09861 (EMNLP 2017, venue from memory). VERIFIED
      (abstract). Says: seed alone produces statistically significant (p < 1e-4) differences, ~1 F1 point
      for NER; recommends comparing score distributions over multiple runs, not single scores.

T2-4  Saravanan, Berman, Sober (2020). "Application of the Hierarchical Bootstrap to Multi-Level Data in
      Neuroscience." Neurons, Behavior, Data Analysis, and Theory 3(5), 2020; bioRxiv 10.1101/819334;
      PMC7906290; also arXiv:2007.07797. VERIFIED (search results show PubMed/PMC/bioRxiv records with
      matching metadata; abstract content summarised from those records). Says: nested data (e.g.
      neurons within animals) are not independent; resampling level by level keeps Type-I error at its
      nominal rate while retaining more power than summarise-first methods. Direct methodological
      precedent for our two-level (maps x entity clusters) bootstrap.
      (Classical source, not opened: Davison & Hinkley 1997 "Bootstrap Methods and their Application",
      Ch. 3.8 on hierarchical data -- UNVERIFIED.)

T2-5  Madaan, Singh, Schaeffer, Poulton, Koyejo, Stenetorp, et al. (2024). "Quantifying Variance in
      Evaluation Benchmarks." arXiv:2406.10229. VERIFIED (abstract).
      Says: measures seed variance across initialisations and other variance metrics for LLM benchmarks;
      urges practitioners to factor variance into model comparisons.

T2-6  Picard (2021). "Torch.manual_seed(3407) is all you need: On the influence of random seeds in deep
      learning architectures for computer vision." arXiv:2109.08203. VERIFIED (abstract).
      Says: scanning up to 1e4 seeds on CIFAR-10, variance is not huge but outlier seeds much better or
      worse than average are easy to find -- i.e. a single draw can be cherry-picked by accident.

T2-7  Henderson, Islam, Bachman, Pineau, Precup, Meger (2018). "Deep Reinforcement Learning that
      Matters." arXiv:1709.06560 (AAAI 2018, venue from memory). VERIFIED (abstract).
      Says: variance intrinsic to methods plus nondeterminism makes reported results hard to interpret
      without significance metrics; gives reporting guidelines.

Random-feature / random-projection variance across draws:

T2-8  Rahimi, Recht (2007). "Random Features for Large-Scale Kernel Machines." NIPS 20 (2007).
      VERIFIED (proceedings page papers.nips.cc/paper/3182 seen in search results; not opened in full).
      Says: random feature maps approximate shift-invariant kernels with convergence bounds -- the
      approximation quality is a random variable of the draw.

T2-9  Sutherland, Schneider (2015). "On the Error of Random Fourier Features." arXiv:1506.02785
      (UAI 2015, venue from memory). VERIFIED (abstract). Says: improves the uniform error bound of
      Rahimi & Recht and analyses the embedding's VARIANCE; the more widely used variant is strictly
      higher-variance for the Gaussian kernel. Best citation for "results depend on the random draw".

T2-10 Dasgupta, Gupta (2003). "An Elementary Proof of a Theorem of Johnson and Lindenstrauss." Random
      Structures & Algorithms 22(1):60-65. VERIFIED (dblp record seen in search; not opened).
      Says: random projections to k >= 24 ln n / (3 eps^2 - 2 eps^3) dims preserve pairwise distances
      within (1 +- eps) with high probability -- concentration, so draw-to-draw variance shrinks with k.

T2-11 Thomas, Dasgupta, Rosing (2021). "A Theoretical Perspective on Hyperdimensional Computing."
      J. Artificial Intelligence Research 72:215-249. arXiv:2010.07426. VERIFIED (abstract+journal_ref).
      Says: unified theory of HD representations (random high-dimensional codes), incl. capacity and
      suitability for learning; the natural VSA-side citation for concentration of random codes.

T2-12 Frady, Kleyko, Sommer (2018). "A Theory of Sequence Indexing and Working Memory in Recurrent
      Neural Networks." arXiv:1803.00412 (Neural Computation 30(6), 2018, venue from memory). VERIFIED
      (abstract). Says: capacity theory for VSA-style storage with randomized input weights; crosstalk
      noise limits retrieval; diverse VSA models share universal performance properties. Supports
      treating map-to-map differences as crosstalk noise with predictable spread.

Not found: a paper that specifically re-draws the random VSA codebook and re-tunes per draw as a
benchmark-variance protocol. T2-1 is the general-ML precedent.

TOPIC 3. Phase-change memory conductance drift: time horizons of the device models
======================================================================

(a) The statistical PCM model papers and the time span their drift data/fits cover

T3-1  Nandakumar, Le Gallo, Boybat, Rajendran, Sebastian, Eleftheriou (2018). "A phase-change memory model
      for neuromorphic computing." J. Appl. Phys. 124, 152135. DOI 10.1063/1.5042408. VERIFIED (full
      preprint text, Zenodo record 1492261).
      Drift time span: 10,000 devices (90 nm mushroom cells, doped GST); after each partial-SET pulse an
      immediate read at ~100 ns, then 50 reads "at time intervals in the order of seconds" (each read of
      the 10k devices ~1 s; average 38.6 s between pulses). Fig. 4 time axis ~1e-7 to 1e2 s.
      Drift fitted with G(t) = G(T0) (t/T0)^-nu, mean nu = 0.04, nu decreasing with conductance; each
      partial SET re-initiates drift. => This model's drift fit covers ~100 ns to ~tens of seconds only.

T3-2  Joshi, Le Gallo, Haefeli, Boybat, Nandakumar, Piveteau, et al. (2020). "Accurate deep neural network
      inference using computational phase-change memory." Nature Communications 11, 2473. arXiv:1906.03138.
      VERIFIED (full arXiv text).
      Drift time span: weights first measured ~25 s after programming, then "periodically read from
      hardware over a period of 1 day". The behavioural PCM model (Gaussian drift exponent with mean and
      SD depending on target conductance; 1/f^1.21 read noise growing with time) was "built based on an
      extensive experimental characterization" and reproduces the array statistics "over the duration of
      the experiment" (one day). The authors THEMSELVES extrapolate with the model to one year:
      "This improvement becomes 1.8% for one year when extrapolating the results using the PCM model";
      ImageNet weight evolution "simulate[d] ... for one year". => fitted on ~25 s to ~1 day; one-year
      numbers are model extrapolations, not measurements.

T3-3  Rasch, Moreda, Gokmen, Le Gallo, Carta, Goldberg, et al. (2021). "A flexible and fast PyTorch toolkit
      for simulating training and inference on analog crossbar arrays" (IBM aihwkit). arXiv:2104.02184
      (IEEE AICAS 2021, venue from memory). VERIFIED (full arXiv text).
      Says: the inference tile's "noise strength and temporal profile is calibrated on a 1M phase-change
      memory (PCM) device array [Joshi et al.]"; Fig. 3C compares model vs experiment on a time axis of
      ~1e2 to 1e5 s (about one day); small model/experiment mismatch below ~1000 s due to programming
      jitter. The aihwkit source docstring of PCMLikeNoiseModel (read online) says the model "was fitted
      and characterized on real PCM devices", cites Nandakumar et al. ICECS 2019, uses t_0 = 20 s as
      "first reading time", and states NO validity range or caveat about extrapolating t_inference.
      => If our hardware comparison used aihwkit's PCMLikeNoiseModel, its fit window is ~20 s to ~1 day.

T3-4  Nandakumar, Boybat, Joshi, Piveteau, Le Gallo, Rajendran, Sebastian, Eleftheriou (2019).
      "Phase-change memory models for deep learning training and inference." IEEE ICECS 2019.
      UNVERIFIED (title seen in EPFL Infoscience search result and cited by the aihwkit docstring; not
      opened). Likely the direct source of aihwkit's PCMLikeNoiseModel parameters.

T3-5  Boybat, Le Gallo, Nandakumar, Moraitis, Parnell, Tuma, et al. (2018). "Neuromorphic computing with
      multi-memristive synapses." Nature Communications 9, 2514. arXiv:1711.06507. VERIFIED (abstract).
      Says: comprehensive PCM model + experiments on >1M PCM devices. (Search snippet, UNVERIFIED: a
      related Boybat/Nandakumar conference paper reports nu depends on conductance and drift history is
      erased by partial SET pulses -- consistent with T3-1.)

(b) Does the power law hold over long horizons (days to years)?

T3-6  Le Gallo, Krebs, Zipoli, Salinga, Sebastian (2018). "Collective structural relaxation in
      phase-change memory devices." Advanced Electronic Materials 4, 1700627 (article number from memory).
      VERIFIED (full IBM preprint text).
      Says: "the log(t) kinetics have been observed over a wide range of time (from ~100 ns up to
      months)"; their collective-relaxation model matches drift measurements over 10 decades of time at
      160-420 K; "At room temperature, the model yields the logarithmic law from 100 ns ... up to 10
      years"; "no saturation of the temporal evolution of the electrical conductivity of phase-change
      materials has yet been observed experimentally", saturation time exceeding crystallisation time.
      => SUPPORTS extrapolating the power-law FORM to 30 days at room temperature (for the amorphous /
      RESET state of doped GST). It does not validate the per-device nu DISTRIBUTION or the 1/f noise
      growth of a statistical model fitted on 1 day, nor intermediate (partial-SET) states, nor elevated
      temperature (drift accelerates with T).

T3-7  Kersting, Sarwat, Le Gallo, Brew, Walfort, Saulnier, et al. (2021). "Measurement of onset of
      structural relaxation in melt-quenched phase change materials." arXiv:2106.06270. VERIFIED
      (abstract). Says: the logarithmic time dependence "is valid only in a certain observable
      timescale"; they measure the ONSET of drift at short times. The deviation documented is at the
      short-time end, not at days-to-months.

T3-8  Kersting (2024). "Quest for a solution to drift in phase change memory devices." PhD thesis,
      arXiv:2401.09462. VERIFIED (abstract). Says: drift measured over 9 orders of magnitude in time;
      appraises Gibbs vs collective relaxation models; state-dependent drift in projected cells.

T3-9  Pries et al. (2022). Adv. Funct. Mater. (title not captured). UNVERIFIED (search snippet only).
      Snippet: drift is dominated by glass dynamics; ageing moves the glass toward equilibrium so drift
      should eventually level off; proposes an "extended power law". Do not cite without opening.

T3-10 Elliott (Cambridge repository), "Electronic mechanism for resistance drift in phase-change memory
      materials: link to persistent photoconductivity." UNVERIFIED (search snippet only). Snippet: drift
      follows a weak power law over at least 14 orders of magnitude in time starting from ~30 ns.

(c) Error-correcting codes vs distributed / hyperdimensional storage on noisy hardware

T3-11 Kim, Hun-Seok (2018). "HDM: Hyper-Dimensional Modulation for Robust Low-Power Communications."
      IEEE ICC 2018, DOI 10.1109/ICC.2018.8422472. VERIFIED (full PDF text).
      Says: "uncoded HDM with 256-dimension exhibits the bit error rate (BER) comparable to that of
      low-density parity check (LDPC) and Polar codes, while HDM demodulation complexity is lower ...
      for the same block length of 256"; a CRC-based correction adds 1.75 dB. The only DIRECT
      HD-vs-ECC head-to-head found -- but on a noisy wireless channel, not on NVM.

T3-12 Karunaratne, Le Gallo, Cherubini, Benini, Rahimi, Sebastian (2020). "In-memory hyperdimensional
      computing." Nature Electronics 3, 327-337 (venue/pages from memory). arXiv:1906.01548. VERIFIED
      (abstract). Says: HDC's robustness to substrate imperfections makes it suited to in-memory
      computing; complete in-memory HDC system on 760,000 PCM devices reaches accuracies comparable to
      software. No ECC baseline.

T3-13 Kleyko, Davies, Frady, Kanerva, Kent, Olshausen, et al. (2022). "Vector Symbolic Architectures as a
      Computing Framework for Emerging Hardware." Proceedings of the IEEE 110(10). arXiv:2106.05268.
      VERIFIED (abstract+journal_ref). Says: VSA is well suited to stochastic emerging hardware and
      computing in superposition; reference review for the robustness argument. No ECC comparison.

T3-14 Langenegger, Karunaratne, Hersche, Benini, Sebastian, Rahimi (2023). "In-memory factorization of
      holographic perceptual representations." arXiv:2211.05052 (Nature Nanotechnology 2023, venue from
      memory). VERIFIED (abstract). Says: uses the intrinsic stochasticity of PCM in-memory compute as a
      resource for a holographic (HDC) factorizer on two PCM chips.

T3-15 HDC-resilience papers on SRAM/FeFET (par.nsf.gov hits; COSIME arXiv:2207.12188). UNVERIFIED
      (snippets only). Snippets claim ECC is "not cost-effective due to the large implementation
      overhead" and cite a CIM-SECDED scheme sacrificing 20% of memory cells; HDC tolerates ~10% device
      error. Not opened; do not cite as is.

Not found: a controlled comparison of ECC-protected conventional storage vs distributed/holographic (HD)
storage on PCM/RRAM bit-error models at equal bit budget. Our hardware check would be the first such
comparison we know of -- but phrase as "we found no direct comparison", not as a novelty claim.

BOTTOM LINE FOR B52
======================================================================
T1: Mid-layer > final-layer for identity is well supported (T1-1, T1-2, T1-3; T1-7 for multi-token
    units). Digit-wise number codes (T1-8) fit our decimal result. Anisotropy nuance: decoders are MOST
    anisotropic at mid depth (T1-17), so apply centring/whitening at layers 9/27 too.
T2: Re-drawing maps and re-selecting config per map is exactly Bouthillier et al.'s recommendation
    (T2-1); two-level bootstrap = hierarchical bootstrap (T2-4); random-feature variance (T2-9, T2-10).
T3: The aihwkit / Joshi 2020 PCM model is fitted on ~20-25 s to ~1 day (1e2-1e5 s) of 1M-device data
    (T3-2, T3-3); the older Nandakumar 2018 JAP model's drift fit spans only ~100 ns to tens of seconds
    (T3-1). Joshi et al. themselves extrapolate to one year with the model, and Le Gallo 2018 reports
    log(t) drift observed up to months with no saturation (T3-6) -- so a 30-day number is a defensible
    MODEL EXTRAPOLATION of the mean drift law, but the device-statistics (nu spread, 1/f growth) are
    only validated to ~1 day; reporting the 1-day number as primary and 30 days as extrapolation is the
    conservative choice.
